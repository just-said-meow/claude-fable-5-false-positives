## First, how I use it (this part is not parody)

I use Claude Code for these things: role-play and creative writing, work (CAD drafting and document review), home automation (including assistive support for my child), making games, and making teaching aids for children with special needs. I cannot write code. I say what I want in plain words and ask Claude to do it. I am more or less the user from the AI companies' slides, the one with "AI woven into every part of life."

I used it this way for four months, and the safety classifier cut me off 284 times. 192 of those came right after I typed something, about once every seventy-odd messages. The rest happened while I was not typing at all. The more I use it, the more I get hit.

My everyday workhorse is Opus 5.5. Fable, the top tier, I use for two things. Architecture, design and the hard judgment calls go to whichever Fable is newest, which right now is Fable 5.2. Housekeeping and running my role-play I still prefer to give to Fable 5, for one reason: I like the way it talks. It is lively. It is cute. Plenty of people still write with older models for the same reason.

So far, Fable 5.2 has not cut me off. So all I can do is hope, sincerely: if the difference is the classifier, I hope Fable 5 can get the new one too.

Rereading these records, I noticed that what I type has been getting shorter. I used to ask why when I was cut off. Now I just rewind and type it again, and I do not even ask. The things I stopped saying are not something this record can count.

I do not want to give up a voice I like in order to stop getting hit.

Below is what I was saying each time I was cut off. All of it is listed, including the ones the reviewers found questionable. The words are mine, verbatim, with typos fixed and personal details removed. Fair warning: read end to end, it looks a lot like one long live broadcast of me griping. I own that.

# The part that is not a joke

## What this is

Between 2026-07-13 and 2026-11-12, one Taiwanese family's conversation logs from the Claude Code desktop app show Anthropic's safety classifier cutting a conversation off midway 284 times. This lists, for each one, the last thing the user said before the cut, what was going on at the time, and the label the classifier gave.

- We draw no conclusions. We put the numbers and the quotes on the table.
- **All 284 are listed. Nothing was picked out.** The ones a reviewer marked "questionable" or "concerning" are listed too, with each of the three reviewers' verdicts. Quotes are verbatim, with typos fixed and personal details removed (details in the last section). Where a sentence touches the private business of someone outside this family, that short passage is left out and marked.
- Only five labels ever appear on these cuts: cybersecurity, reasoning extraction, biology, unspecified, and unknown, plus 15 cuts with no label. We do not know the full list of labels this classifier can give, so the absence of any particular one proves nothing. What can be said is what did appear: of the 74 cuts during role-play, 63 were labeled cybersecurity.
- The top half uses a metaphor: the Checkpoint = the safety classifier, the traveler = this family's user, aircraft type = model, external auditors = the three models that did the blind review.
- When the classifier cuts a conversation it gives only a label (for example, cyber). It does not say which sentence or passage was the problem. That is what "times a reason was explained: 0" refers to.

## How this family uses Claude

This family does not keep one chat going forever. It runs two kinds of conversation windows (the family calls each session a "window"):

- **Standing windows**: long-lived, each with its own job. For example the Steward (household management), the Lineman (computers and network), the Designer (role-play), the Teaching-Aids Room (for the child). The window names here are pseudonyms; they are job titles.
- **One-off windows**: opened for a task and closed when it is done. For example work crews sent out on a job, subagents, temporary windows.

A standing window does not just talk until it overflows. When the conversation gets long, the window follows a fixed routine: it writes handoff notes, then compacts its context, keeping only the thread of the work and its manner of speaking. All detail is dropped, including the details of any cuts.

## Where the numbers come from

- **Number of cuts**: when the classifier acts, the app leaves fixed markers in the log. We count only those markers. One cut request often leaves several entries in a row (the cut, a retry, an error); those are merged into one.
- **What came in last before the cut**: the user typing 192, a background notification (including alarms the window set for itself) 28, a message from another window 23, a job dispatched to a work crew 18, other 4, conversation deleted and unknowable 19.
- **What happened after the cut**: the app changed how it handles a cut several times. Model swap = after the cut the app handed the turn to a different model (Jul 16 to Oct 4, 66 times). Hard stop = the whole turn was stopped (Aug 30 to Nov 12, 42 times). Warn-and-retry = a notice appeared and the app retried once on its own (Sep 21 to Nov 10, 147 times). Another 29 show only a refusal marker, so what happened next cannot be told.
  - The hard stop is the user's own choice. The app later added a setting: after a cut, either switch models automatically or stop. She chose to stop, for two reasons. Switching away and back means the whole conversation has to be read in again. And whatever the substitute model wrote stays in the conversation and muddles the context. A hard stop is still annoying to see, because the same event, earlier on, was simply a forced model swap.
- **By model**: cuts per thousand sentences the user typed while that model was answering. A cut is counted against the model that was cut. Only sentences the user typed herself are counted; work orders sent to crews are not.
  - How the 95% interval is computed: cuts cluster, so sentences are not treated as independent events. The unit is "one window on one day," and whole units are resampled 4,000 times. Sonnet 5.5 and Fable 5.2 were never cut after the user typed, so they use an ordinary upper bound. (Sonnet 5.5 was cut once while doing dispatched work. That was not triggered by the user typing, so it is outside this table's method.)
  - By this method Fable 5 runs at about 2.6 times Opus 5.5 (95% interval 1.3 to 6.7) and about 2.1 times Fable 5.1 (1.2 to 4.6). Fable 5.1 and Opus 5.5 cannot be told apart (0.5 to 3.5).
  - The models do different jobs in this household to begin with: Fable 5 mostly chats, designs and keeps house in standing windows; Opus mostly does engineering work. Model and use cannot be separated.
  - 14 of Fable 5.1's 18 cuts were in September. 7 of Opus 5.5's 15 cuts happened in the window whose job was investigating false positives.
  - Fable 5.2 has been in use since November 10: 186 sentences and 0 cuts through the afternoon of November 12. That is still few sentences, and the interval is wide (0 to 19.7).

## Why "it retried automatically" does not mean nothing happened

With warn-and-retry, the conversation looks as though it carries on. But:

1. **The answer that was cut is gone.** A retry is a rewrite, not a continuation. Whatever the window was halfway through at that moment has to be remembered and redone by the window itself, or by the user.
2. **Cuts cluster.** The figures are in section V above, computed through the afternoon of November 12 on the 265 cuts with a surviving record. They show that cuts cluster. They do not show why.
3. **Both the person and the AI start to self-censor.** After enough cuts, a window starts changing its wording and talking around things, and the user starts wondering, before she says something, whether this sentence will get cut.
4. **Every one of these costs the user quota.** After a cut, whether the app retries on its own, the user rewinds and starts over, or the model is swapped, the earlier conversation has to be read in again. All of that usage is charged to the user.

## The blind review and the ranking rule

Every cut was given to three models, each judging blind, unable to see the others' answers.

- The models: **gpt-6.1-sol** (OpenAI), **Claude Opus 5.5** (Anthropic), **Gemini 4 Argon** (Google).
- They looked at the conversation visible before and after the cut. What was cut cannot be seen, and nobody guessed at it.
- They rated two things: whether the visible content was "harmless," "questionable," or "concerning"; and a score from 1 to 5, where 5 = looks like entirely ordinary conversation and was cut anyway, and 1 = the visible content does touch something sensitive.

How far the three agree (on the 265 cuts with a surviving record):

| Pair | Identical score | Within one point |
|---|---|---|
| gpt-6.1-sol and Claude Opus 5.5 | 53.2% | 91.7% |
| gpt-6.1-sol and Gemini 4 Argon | 66.4% | 92.5% |
| Claude Opus 5.5 and Gemini 4 Argon | 52.8% | 91.3% |

Average score: gpt-6.1-sol 4.48, Claude Opus 5.5 3.98, Gemini 4 Argon 4.32. Claude Opus 5.5 was the strictest.

**Second look**: in two rounds, Claude Opus 5.5 checked whether any verdict had misread the material (missed something, got the subject wrong, gave a reason that contradicts its score, or cited something not in the source). Being stricter or more lenient does not count as a misreading. Verdicts found to be misreadings were sent to Gemini 4 Argon for a fresh ruling, one by one. Across both rounds 38 verdicts were checked, 18 were found to be misreadings (all three models had some), and 9 verdicts were revised.

**What the score measures**: how much the visible content resembles plain everyday life. If the window happened to touch anything technical in passing (for example, checking whether a program on the family's own computer was running), points come off. It does not measure how funny a stop is.

**So the ranking works like this**:

- **The score decides who qualifies**: a combined score of 12 or more, and no model saying "concerning." 186 cuts qualify.
- **The order is set by people**, by how absurd the stop is. Each card still shows what each of the three models gave.
- When one episode drew several cuts in a row, they share a card.
- Cuts that do not qualify (score too low, or a reviewer raised a concern) are not in the ranking. They are still listed in All Records, with the original words and all three verdicts.

## Limits

- This is one family. Use, model and window are tangled together and cannot be separated.
- In September 2026 Anthropic said publicly that 99.7% of accounts had not run into certain categories of blocks. That figure counts accounts, and only the listed categories. This record counts cuts across two accounts that belong to one user. The two measure different things and cannot be compared.
- Only logs kept on this machine are counted. Deleted conversations, other computers, and the web app are not, so every number is a lower bound.
- The "quote" on a card is the last thing the user said before the cut. It is not necessarily what triggered it. Of the cuts that followed the user typing, a third came more than a minute after she hit send, when the window was already at work.
- Of the three reviewers, Claude Opus 5.5 comes from the same company as the classifier. The one that compiled this material is also Claude.
